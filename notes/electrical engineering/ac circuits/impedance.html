Electrical Impedance is the AC equivalent of resistance in DC circuits.

It is denoted by the symbol $z$ and is measured in ohms ($\Omega$).

It is a complex number that can be expressed as $z = R + jX$, where $R$ is the resistance and $X$ is the reactance.

We use the symbol $j$ instead of $i$ to avoid confusion with current.

We can treat components with impedance (resistors, inductors, and capacitors) as resistance

This means we can add them in series and parallel the same way as resistance

We can also use voltage and current dividers

We can also use Ohm's law $$V = I \cdot Z$$

The formulas for impedance are:

Resistor: $z_R = R$

Inductor: $z_L = j\omega L$ = $j2\pi fL$

Capacitor: $z_C = \frac{1}{j\omega C}$ = $\frac{1}{j2\pi fC}$ = $-\frac{j}{2\pi fC}$

Where $\omega$ is the angular frequency of the AC signal, $\omega = 2\pi f$, where $f$ is the frequency in hertz (Hz).

And R is the resistance. L is the inductance. C is the capacitance.


Commonly rectangular form is used for impedance addition or subtraction.

Polar Form is used for impedance multiplication or division, or analysis of AC circuits.

We typically write polar form using phasors: $Z = |Z|e^{j\theta} (\text{polar form})$ or $Z = |Z| \angle \theta (\text{phasor form})$

Motivation

If we have a simple AC circuit, with just an AC source and a resistor, the current is simply a scaled version of the voltage.

However, if we have an inductor or a capacitor in the circuit, the curernt is also scaled, but it also phase-shifted

The reason for this is because voltage and current through these components are related by differential equations

For a 1F capacitor,

$$\begin{align} I(t) &= C \frac{dV(t)}{dt} \\ &= 1 \cdot \frac{d(5\sin(2\pi t))}{dt} \\ &= 10\pi \cos(2\pi t) \\ &= 10\pi \sin\left(2\pi t + \frac{\pi}{2}\right) \end{align}$$

For a 1H inductor,

$$\begin{align} V(t) &= L \frac{dI(t)}{dt} \\ 5\sin(2\pi t) &= 1 \cdot \frac{dI(t)}{dt} \\ I(t) &= \int 5\sin(2\pi t) \, dt \\ &= -\frac{5}{2\pi}\cos(2\pi t) + C\\ &= \frac{5}{2\pi}\sin\left(2\pi t - \frac{\pi}{2}\right) \end{align}$$

We can ignore the constant of integration $C$, because it represents a DC component

The DC component decays away over time, and we are typically interested in the steady-state response.

Notice how the current is not in phase with the voltage (because of the $\pm \frac{\pi}{2}$)

Also, the frequency of the current is the same as the voltage, so that remains unchanged.

Generalizing and Derivation

For any voltage values, and component values, assume voltage is $V(t) = V_m \sin(\omega t + \phi) = V_m \sin(2\pi ft + \phi)$

$V_m$ is the amplitude, $\omega$ is the angular frequency, $f$ is the frequency, and $\phi$ is the phase angle.

Then, the current through a resistor is $I_R(t) = \frac{V_m}{R} \sin(\omega t + \phi)$

The current through an inductor is $I_L(t) = \frac{V_m}{\omega L}\sin\left(\omega t + \phi - \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$

The current through a capacitor is $I_C(t) = \omega C V_m \sin\left(\omega t + \phi + \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$

If we have a circuit with multiple of these components, we could use differential equations to solve it

Or can use an easier system that makes it an algebraic problem, which is our complex impedance approach

Derivation Using Euler's Formula

If our voltage source is $V(t) = V_m \cos(\omega t + \phi)$, and $e^{j(\omega t + \phi)} = \cos(\omega t + \phi) + j\sin(\omega t + \phi)$

Then $V(t) = \Re\{V_m e^{j(\omega t + \phi)}\}$, we can do our process without the $\Re$, and just get the real part at the end

The reason why the voltage here is cosine instead of sine is because it's a more convenient form for the analysis

They are equivalent if you take phase into account $\cos(x) = \sin(x + \frac{\pi}{2})$

Taking derivatives of complex numbers works the same, just treat as a constant

Capacitor:

$$\begin{align} I(t) &= C \frac{dV(t)}{dt} \\ &= C \frac{dV_m e^{j(\omega t + \phi)}}{dt} \\ &= j\omega C V_m e^{j(\omega t + \phi)} \\ &= j\omega C V(t)\\ Z_C &= \frac{V(t)}{I(t)} = \frac{1}{j\omega C} \end{align}$$

Inductor:

Since voltage is sinusoidal, current is also sinusoidal same frequency, but different amplitude and phase, $I(t) = I_m e^{j(\omega t + \phi_c)}$

$$\begin{align} V(t) &= L \frac{dI(t)}{dt} \\ &= L \frac{dI_m e^{j(\omega t + \phi_c)}}{dt} \\ &= j\omega L I_m e^{j(\omega t + \phi_c)} \\ &= j\omega L I(t)\\ Z_L &= \frac{V(t)}{I(t)} = j\omega L \end{align}$$

Derivation Using Fourier Transform

If our voltage source is $V(t) = V_m \cos(\omega t + \phi)$, then current is similar form $I(t) = I_m \cos(\omega t + \phi_c)$

The only thing that changes is the amplitude and phase

If the amplitude and phase stay constant, but the frequency changes, the impedance will also change

This means our impedance is a function of frequency

We can use a fourier transform to convert our time-domain signals to the frequency domain

We can use the derivative property of the Fourier transform:

$$\begin{align} \mathcal{F}\left\{a\frac{d}{dt}f(t)\right\} &= aj\omega F(\omega) \\ \end{align}$$

Resistor: $V(t) = RI(t) \rightarrow V(\omega) = \mathcal{F}\left\{R I(\omega)\right\} = R I(\omega) \rightarrow Z_R = R$

Capacitor: $I(t) = C \frac{dV(t)}{dt} \rightarrow I(\omega) = \mathcal{F}\left\{C V(\omega)\right\} = j\omega C V(\omega) \rightarrow Z_C = \frac{1}{j\omega C}$

Inductor: $V(t) = L \frac{dI(t)}{dt} \rightarrow V(\omega) = \mathcal{F}\left\{L I(\omega)\right\} = j\omega L I(\omega) \rightarrow Z_L = j\omega L$

Properties

Since we can treat inductance as resistance, we can add in series and parallel the same way

Since we can treat inductance as resistance, we can use voltage and current dividers the same way

$$\begin{align} Z_{eq} = Z_1 + Z_2 + ... + Z_n \ \text{(series)} \\ \frac{1}{Z_{eq}} = \frac{1}{Z_1} + \frac{1}{Z_2} + ... + \frac{1}{Z_n} \ \text{(parallel)} \\ V_1 = V \cdot \frac{Z_1}{Z_1 + Z_2} \ \text{(voltage divider)} \\ I_1 = I \cdot \frac{Z_2}{Z_1 + Z_2} \ \text{(current divider)} \end{align}$$

Example Circuit

Find the equivalent impedance of the circuit shown, and find the voltage across each element.

First find the individual impedances:

$$\begin{align} Z_R &= 150 \Omega \\ Z_C &= \frac{-j}{\omega C} = \frac{-j}{2 \cdot \pi \cdot 50 \cdot 68 \times 10^{-9}} \approx -j46810.277 \Omega \\ Z_L &= j\omega L = j \cdot 2 \cdot \pi \cdot 50 \cdot 10 \times 10^{-6} \approx .0031j\Omega \end{align}$$

Then find the equivalent impedance (they are in series):

$$\begin{align} Z_{eq} &= Z_R + Z_C + Z_L \\ &= 150 - j46810.277 + .0031j \\ &\approx 150 - j46810.274 \Omega \end{align}$$

To find the voltage across each element, use the voltage divider rule:

$$\begin{align} V_R &= V \cdot \frac{Z_R}{Z_{eq}} \\ V_C &= V \cdot \frac{Z_C}{Z_{eq}} \\ V_L &= V \cdot \frac{Z_L}{Z_{eq}} \end{align}$$

Since we are dividing complex numbers, it will be easier if we convert to polar form for all our components:

$$\begin{align} Z_R &= 150 \angle 0^\circ \\ Z_C &\approx 46810.277 \angle -90^\circ \\ Z_L &\approx 0.0031 \angle 90^\circ \\ Z_{eq} &\approx 46810.5 \angle -89.82^\circ \end{align}$$

Then we can do the division easily:

$$\begin{align} V_R &\approx 5 \angle 0^\circ \cdot \frac{150 \angle 0^\circ}{46810.5 \angle -89.82^\circ} \\ &\approx 0.016 \angle 89.82^\circ \ V \\ V_C &\approx 5 \angle 0^\circ \cdot \frac{46810.277 \angle -90^\circ}{46810.5 \angle -89.82^\circ} \\ &\approx 4.999 \angle -0.18^\circ \ V \\ V_L &\approx 5 \angle 0^\circ \cdot \frac{0.0031 \angle 90^\circ}{46810.5 \angle -89.82^\circ} \\ &\approx 3.3\times10^{-7} \angle 179.82^\circ \ V \end{align}$$

Analysis

We can see the voltage in the inductor leads the current by 90 degrees.

We can see the voltage in the capacitor lags the current by 90 degrees.

This is a common saying called ELI The ICE Man, where E stans for electromotive force (voltage)

L stands for inductance, I stands for current, and C stands for capacitance

ELI means voltage leads current in an inductor, and ICE means current leads voltage in a capacitor
